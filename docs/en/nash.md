# Nash — The equilibrium behind collective behaviour

## Why this encoding?

Nash's game theory solves a problem neither Adam Smith nor Machiavelli really tackles: how do rational agents behave *when they anticipate what the others will do*? Smith reads incentive mechanisms in a market. Machiavelli maps the power dynamics around a single decision-maker. Nash models situations where each party's outcome depends on everyone's choices, and looks for the point of stability, the equilibrium, which isn't necessarily the best possible result. The encoding produces a map of forces, not a prescription.

## What the method corrects

When someone says "everyone has an interest in cooperating" or "nobody understands why it's stuck", the method asks: what is the Nash equilibrium here? A system can stay in a sub-optimal state not because the players are foolish or ill-intentioned, but because that state is stable — none of them can leave it alone without ending up worse off. It corrects the confusion between what's collectively desirable and what's individually stable.

## When to invoke it

- When a situation seems stuck and nobody understands why no one moves
- On decisions where the outcome depends on what other players do (competition, negotiation, standards)
- To anticipate how adversaries or partners will react to a strategic move
- When you want to design a rule or mechanism that changes behaviour — not just recommend it

## When not to invoke it

- When the players aren't rational, or their motives are largely non-economic
- On purely individual decisions with no strategic dimension (no other players whose choices shape the result)
- When what you know about the other players' payoffs is too thin to model anything useful

## Use cases

### 1. A price war in a duopoly

**Context:** Two dominant players keep cutting prices, eroding both their margins.

**Input:** "We're in a price war with our main competitor — nobody's winning, but nobody stops."

**What the skill produces:** "Players: you and the competitor. Strategies: hold the price, cut the price. Payoffs: raise alone and you lose share; both hold and margins are acceptable. Nash equilibrium: cut, because whatever the other does, cutting is the safer individual bet. Collective optimum: both hold — and nobody does, because it isn't the equilibrium. Ways out: a public signal of intent (a price announcement), coordination through an industry standard, or a merger. Without a mechanism, the equilibrium doesn't move."

### 2. Adopting a technical standard

**Context:** Several players in a market hesitate to adopt a new protocol — each waits for the others to move first.

**Input:** "Everyone agrees the standard would be better for the ecosystem, but nobody wants to migrate first."

**What the skill produces:** "The game: adopt or wait. Payoffs: migrating alone is costly in development and compatibility; migrating once others have is cheaper; staying put once others have moved means isolation. Current equilibrium: wait (the dominant short-term strategy). Collective optimum: migrate together. The gap comes from the cost of being first. Ways out: a coordinated commitment (consortium), a subsidy for the first mover, or a player big enough to make migration pay on its own and pull the others along."

### 3. Pay negotiation within a team

**Context:** Employees know they could negotiate collectively, but each fears being the only one to ask.

**Input:** "Everyone thinks they're underpaid but nobody asks for a raise — so we all stay in the same spot."

**What the skill produces:** "Players: each employee individually. Strategies: ask, don't ask. Payoffs: asking alone feels risky (refusal, awkwardness, retaliation); not asking keeps the status quo. Nash equilibrium: don't ask (everyone waits for someone else to move). Collective optimum: a coordinated negotiation or pay transparency. The way out is changing the rules of the game — pay transparency among colleagues or collective representation — not an individual incentive to take the risk."

## Limits of this encoding

Rationality is a model, not a description of the world — people make sub-optimal choices for emotional, ideological or informational reasons, and the skill doesn't capture that. Modelling the payoffs is often the hardest part: what players really optimise (reputation, avoiding conflict, loyalty) is rarely observable directly. The skill works best on two- or three-player games with clearly bounded strategies; real situations are usually messier. Pair it with Adam Smith for incentive analysis, and with Machiavelli for power dynamics that don't reduce to a stable equilibrium.
